\begin{abstract}
Observation times in longitudinal clinical trials are usually
spaced equally for administrative convenience. Optimal design
theory shows that their placement affects the precision of
treatment effect estimates. This paper compares an equally spaced
five-visit schedule with a boundary-clustered schedule. The
setting is a two-arm, 24-month trial with a linearly divergent
treatment effect and a random-intercept, random-slope model. With
complete, balanced data, the variance of the estimated slope
difference has a simple closed form. The schedule enters only
through the corrected sum of squares of the visit times. The
relative efficiency of the clustered schedule is 1.06 at the
base-case parameters. The unconstrained optimum gives 1.12, and
the best schedule with a two-month minimum gap gives 1.08. Under
compound symmetry the clustered schedule gives 1.38. The advantage
shrinks as the random-slope variance grows, but it cannot reverse.
A paired Monte Carlo study with 2000 replications gave an
empirical relative efficiency of 1.07 (95\% bootstrap interval
1.06 to 1.09). It also showed close to nominal coverage for
REML-based inference. Monotone dropout, completely at random and
with a constant hazard, was also modeled. Equal spacing is then
more efficient for cumulative dropout above about 27\%. The small
complete-data gain from clustering should therefore not be relied
on without a dropout calculation. R functions for every
calculation are provided in an accompanying compendium.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

In longitudinal clinical trials, subjects are measured
repeatedly over a fixed study period. The conventional approach
places observation times at equally spaced intervals (monthly or
quarterly visits, for example). This choice largely reflects
administrative convenience rather than statistical efficiency. A
growing body of work in optimal experimental design suggests that
visit placement can substantially affect the precision of
estimated treatment effects. We begin in Section
\ref{optimal-allocation-of-observation-times} with a review of
the relevant literature. We then describe the analytic efficiency
calculation and the simulation study, and their results, in
Sections \ref{methods} and \ref{results}.

\subsection{Optimal allocation of observation times}\label{optimal-allocation-of-observation-times}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkensOptimalTimepointsClinical2005}{1}} provided the foundational
analysis of this problem for two-group trials with linearly
divergent treatment effects. They showed that the optimal
allocation of time-points depends critically on the assumed
covariance structure. Under compound symmetry (CS), they report a
large gain in efficiency from adding repeated measures at the end
of the study. Under a first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) structure, only two
measurements, at baseline and at study end, are needed for
highly efficient estimation. Under a more realistic AR(1) plus
measurement error structure, the optimal spacing depends on the
specific covariance parameter values. Even so, the equally spaced
design is generally suboptimal.

In a companion paper,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkensOptimalNumberRepeated2006}{2}} extended
these results to the joint optimization of the number of repeated
measures and group sizes under budget constraints. They found
that two repeated measures per subject often yield highly
efficient treatment effect estimators. This holds when the budget
is fixed and the covariance structure includes an autoregressive
component. Otherwise, intermediate measures can reduce the
variance of the generalized least squares (GLS) estimator,
particularly under compound symmetry.

\subsection{Design criteria and cost considerations}\label{design-criteria-and-cost-considerations}

The formal framework for these results relies on classical
optimal design theory, as developed by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-atkinson1992optimum}{3}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ouwens2002maximin}{4}} proposed maximin D-optimal designs for
longitudinal mixed effects models. They computed optimal
allocations of time-points for polynomial trend models with
random intercepts, random slopes, and AR(1) serial correlations.
D-optimal designs depend on unknown parameter values. The maximin
criterion therefore provides robustness by maximizing the
worst-case relative efficiency across the parameter space.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-berger2009introduction}{5}} give a textbook treatment of these
criteria for social and biomedical studies.

For the comparison of slopes between two groups, the design
question has a simple structure.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raudenbush2001effects}{6}} studied
how study duration, frequency of observation, and sample size
affect power for group differences in polynomial change.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-galbraith2002guidelines}{7}} gave design guidelines for clinical
trials with longitudinal outcomes.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-zhao2022power}{8}} give related power
formulas for mixed models with random slope and intercept. We
derive the slope variance for the model studied here directly in
Section \ref{analytic-relative-efficiency}. We have not checked
the full texts of these papers against that derivation.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tekle2008doptimal}{9}} extended this work to cohort designs. They
showed that a purely longitudinal design, with a single cohort
measured at optimal time-points, is generally more efficient than
mixed longitudinal-cross-sectional designs. Their numerical
results showed that the most efficient number of repeated
measurements equals the number of cohorts plus the degree of the
polynomial trend. Subsequently,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tekle2011cohorts}{10}} showed that
adding cohorts or repeated measurements beyond this optimum
wastes resources when the study budget is fixed.

\subsection{Practical guidance on the number of measures}\label{practical-guidance-on-the-number-of-measures}

From a more practical perspective,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vickers2003howmany}{11}} examined
the marginal benefit of adding repeated measures in terms of
statistical power. He showed that repeated assessments can have
dramatic effects on power. However, the marginal benefit of each
additional measure decreases rapidly. In the words of his
abstract, `There is little value in increasing the number of
either baseline or post-treatment assessments beyond four, or
seven where baseline assessments are taken.' The exception is
when correlations between measures are low, as in episodic
conditions.

\subsection{Extensions and related work}\label{extensions-and-related-work}

Several extensions of the Winkens framework have appeared.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkens2007polynomial}{12}} generalized the optimal design results to
second-order polynomial treatment effects. They found that
equidistant time-points are either optimal or highly efficient
when only three measures are taken.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-candel2008empirical}{13}}
investigated optimal designs for estimating individual growth
curves (random effects) rather than fixed effects. They showed
that the optimal number of time-points and subjects then differs
from the fixed-effects case.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-abebe2014bayesian}{14}} developed Bayesian
D-optimal designs for dichotomous repeated measures with
autocorrelation. Their designs formally account for parameter
uncertainty through prior distributions.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jones1991unequally}{15}} provided the statistical machinery for the
analysis of unequally spaced longitudinal data. Their methods fit
mixed models with continuous-time AR(1) structures and arbitrary
observation schedules. The approach is based on the Kalman
filter. It remains foundational for analyzing data from designs
with non-equidistant time-points.

\subsection{Power and sample size tools}\label{power-and-sample-size-tools}

Modern software for power and sample size calculations in
longitudinal trials has made these design considerations more
accessible.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-iddi2022longpower}{16}} describe the \texttt{longpower} R
package. It implements closed-form power calculations for linear
mixed models. The package vignette\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2010power}{17}} applies
these calculations to the design of Phase II trials in Alzheimer's
disease.

\subsection{Present study}\label{present-study}

The theoretical optimality results reviewed above establish that
the best design depends on the assumed covariance structure.
However, these results do not by themselves quantify how much
efficiency is at stake for a specific, realistic trial
configuration. Nor do they address the finite-sample behavior of
the REML-based inference that practitioners actually use. This
report addresses that translational gap for one configuration.
That configuration is a 100-patient, two-arm, 24-month trial with
a linearly divergent treatment effect. It is analyzed under a
random-intercept, random-slope model. We compare two five-visit
designs:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Equal spacing: observations at months 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24.
\item
  Clustered spacing: observations clustered near the beginning
  and end of the study period (months 0, 2, 4, 20, 24). Under the
  random-slope model (Section \ref{data-generating-model}),
  boundary-loaded time-points are efficient for estimating the
  slope contrast. The reason is the classical leverage argument.
  Observations far from the mean time carry more information
  about a slope. Section
  \ref{covariance-structure-and-theoretical-prediction} gives
  the derivation.
\end{itemize}

We begin with the exact GLS variance of the slope contrast. For
complete, balanced data under this model it has a one-line closed
form. The schedule enters only through the corrected sum of
squares of the visit times. Compound symmetry is the special case
with no random-slope variance, and the same leverage argument
applies there. The closed form gives the relative efficiency of
any two schedules directly. It also gives the best five-visit
schedule, with and without a minimum gap between visits. We then
extend the calculation to monotone dropout, which completely
observed designs cannot address. Lastly, a common-random-number
Monte Carlo study checks finite-sample REML inference. We examine
bias, empirical versus model-based standard error, power, and 95\%
confidence interval coverage. The simulation cannot change the
efficiency comparison itself, since the variance of the estimator
is known exactly. The functions behind every calculation are in
\texttt{R/} of the accompanying compendium.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model}

We simulate data from a linear mixed effects model with random
intercepts and random slopes. Let \(Y_{ij}\) denote the outcome for
subject \(i\) at time \(t_j\). The generating model is:

\[Y_{ij} = (\beta_0 + b_{0i}) +
         (\beta_1 + b_{1i}) \, t_j +
         \beta_2 \, G_i +
         \beta_3 \, G_i \, t_j +
         \varepsilon_{ij}\]

where \(G_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is the treatment group indicator, and
\(b_{0i}\) and \(b_{1i}\) are subject-specific random effects. These
have \(\text{Var}(b_0) = \tau_0^2\), \(\text{Var}(b_1) = \tau_1^2\),
and \(\text{Cov}(b_0, b_1) = \tau_{01}\). The residuals satisfy
\(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\).

The parameter of interest is \(\beta_3\), the treatment-by-time
interaction. It is the rate at which the treatment effect
diverges from the control effect per unit time.

\subsection{Covariance structure and theoretical prediction}\label{covariance-structure-and-theoretical-prediction}

Marginalizing over the random effects, this model induces a
within-subject covariance
\(\text{Cov}(Y_{ij}, Y_{ij'}) = \tau_0^2 + \tau_{01}(t_j + t_{j'}) +
\tau_1^2 t_j t_{j'} + \sigma^2 \mathbb{1}(j = j')\). The marginal
variance is \(\tau_0^2 + 2\tau_{01} t_j + \tau_1^2 t_j^2 +
\sigma^2\). For \(t_j \geq 0\) it increases with \(t_j\) whenever
\(\tau_{01} \geq 0\), as in our simulations. With \(\tau_{01} < 0\) it
can fall at early visits. This is the standard
random-coefficients (random-intercept, random-slope) covariance
structure. Compound symmetry (CS) is its random-intercept-only
special case (\(\tau_1 = 0\)), with constant correlation regardless
of visit timing. It is distinct from a first-order autoregressive
(AR(1)) structure. Under AR(1), the correlation between two visits
depends only on the lag between them.

We now derive the variance of \(\hat{\beta}_3\). Every subject is
seen at the same times, and the random-effects design
\([\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{t}]\) spans the fixed-effects design within
each arm. In that case the GLS estimator of \(\beta_3\) equals the
difference in arm means of per-subject ordinary least squares
slopes. Each per-subject slope has variance \(\sigma^2 / S_{tt} +
\tau_1^2\), where \(S_{tt} = \sum_j (t_j - \bar{t})^2\) is the
corrected sum of squares of the visit times. In symbols,
\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_3) = \frac{2}{n_g}
  \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{tt}} + \tau_1^2 \right),\]
where \(n_g\) is the number of subjects per arm. Three consequences
follow. First, the schedule matters only through \(S_{tt}\). The
relative efficiency of schedule \(c\) against schedule \(e\) is
\((\sigma^2 / S_e + \tau_1^2) / (\sigma^2 / S_c + \tau_1^2)\). It
exceeds 1 for every \(\tau_1\) whenever \(S_c > S_e\). It shrinks
toward 1 as \(\tau_1^2 S_{tt} / \sigma^2\) grows, but it cannot
reverse. That is, a larger random-slope variance adds a term that
no schedule can reduce. Second, \(\tau_0\) and \(\tau_{01}\) do not
enter, so they play no part in the complete-data comparison.
Third, under CS (\(\tau_1 = 0\)) the relative efficiency is simply
\(S_c / S_e\). The leverage argument is the whole mechanism there
as well. We note that this holds for our mean model, which leaves
the baseline group effect \(\beta_2\) free. A model that constrains
the arms to be equal at baseline would give a different answer.
We have not checked which mean model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkensOptimalTimepointsClinical2005}{1}} used, so we do not compare
our CS result with theirs in detail.

\subsubsection{Choice of the treatment effect}\label{choice-of-the-treatment-effect}

A larger effect, such as \(\beta_3 = 0.5\), would leave power
uninformative. Its analytic power for equal spacing is
\(1 - 3.1 \times 10^{-8}\), and the clustered design is higher still. Power
then cannot discriminate between designs. We therefore chose
\(\beta_3\) so that the equal-spacing design has
about 80\% analytic power at the two-sided \(\alpha = 0.05\) level.
This requires \(\beta_3 / \text{SE}_{\text{equal}} \approx 2.80\),
the sum of the \(z\)-quantiles for 80\% power and a two-sided 5\%
test. With \(\text{SE}_{\text{equal}} = 0.0678\) (Section
\ref{analytic-relative-efficiency}), this gives \(\beta_3 \approx
0.19\). The analytic power at this value is 80.0\% (equal spacing)
and 82.4\% (clustered spacing). These values use normal quantiles.
The fitted models use \(t\) reference distributions with the \texttt{lme}
denominator degrees of freedom. At this sample size the
difference is negligible.

\subsection{Analytic relative efficiency}\label{analytic-relative-efficiency}

Under the data generating model of Section
\ref{data-generating-model}, the GLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}_3\) of
the treatment-by-time interaction has an exact variance for known
\(\mathbf{V}\). Only its use with an estimated \(\mathbf{V}\) is
asymptotic. Let
\(\mathbf{Z}_i = [\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{t}]\) be the \(k \times 2\)
per-subject design matrix for the random effects. Let
\(\mathbf{D}\) be the random-effects covariance matrix, and write
\(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Z}_i \mathbf{D} \mathbf{Z}_i' + \sigma^2
\mathbf{I}_k\). The information matrix is
\(\mathbf{M} = \sum_i \mathbf{X}_i' \mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{X}_i\).
The sum runs separately over control subjects and treatment
subjects. For control subjects the last two columns of
\(\mathbf{X}_i\) are zero. Then
\(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_3) = [\mathbf{M}^{-1}]_{44}\). This
ten-line linear algebra calculation is implemented as
\texttt{analytic\_gls\_se()} in \texttt{R/simulation.R}. It requires no
simulation:

0.0678 (equal spacing) and
0.0658 (clustered spacing).
This gives a relative efficiency (ratio of variances) of

The same values follow from the closed form of Section
\ref{covariance-structure-and-theoretical-prediction}, with
\(S_{tt} = 360\) for equal spacing and
\(S_{tt} = 496\) for clustered
spacing. This analytic value is the primary quantity of interest
for the design comparison.

The simulation in Section \ref{simulation-procedure} answers a
different question. With complete, balanced data the REML point
estimate of \(\beta_3\) is the same difference of per-subject OLS
slopes, whatever the estimated variance components. The package
tests confirm this numerically on simulated datasets. Estimation
error in \(\mathbf{V}\) therefore cannot change the variance of the
point estimate. It affects only the model-based SE and hence Wald
coverage. The simulation is thus a check on inference at
\(n = 100\), and not on efficiency.

\subsection{Scenario extension (analytic only)}\label{scenario-extension-analytic-only}

The paired simulation fixes one covariance structure, one effect
size, and one sample size. The relative efficiency, however, has a
closed form (Section \ref{analytic-relative-efficiency}). We
therefore use \texttt{analytic\_gls\_se\_general()} in \texttt{R/covariance.R},
which accepts an arbitrary within-subject covariance matrix. With
it, the sensitivity of the comparison to the covariance structure
can be assessed without simulation. We vary the random-slope SD
\(\tau_1\) from 0.10 to 0.50. We also consider two further
structures. The first is compound symmetry with \(\tau_0 = 5\) and
\(\sigma = 3\), the \(\tau_1 = 0\) limit of the random-slope model.
The second is AR(1) plus measurement error. Its AR(1) component
has SD 4 and correlation 0.8 per month, and its white-noise SD
is 3. \texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_scenario\_grid.R} regenerates the
results, which are given in Section \ref{analytic-scenario-grid}.

\subsection{Scenario extension (Monte Carlo)}\label{scenario-extension-monte-carlo}

We also ran a Monte Carlo grid that crosses covariance structure
with visit schedule. The structures are the random-slope model
with \(\tau_1 = 0.10\), 0.30, and 0.50, compound symmetry, and AR(1)
plus measurement error, with the parameters given above. We use
four five-visit schedules that span a range of boundary
clustering. Equal spacing uses months 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, as in the
main comparison. Mild clustering uses months 0, 4, 8, 20, 24.
Boundary clustering uses months 0, 2, 4, 20, 24, the `Clustered'
design of the main comparison. Extreme clustering uses months 0,
1, 2, 23, 24. Each of the 20 cells has 1000
replications fitted by REML. The random-slope structures use
\texttt{run\_one\_sim()}. Compound symmetry and AR(1) plus error use
\texttt{run\_one\_sim\_cov()}, which fits \texttt{nlme::gls()} with the correlation
structure that generated the data. Every cell is thus fitted with
the correct covariance family, and no misspecification is
examined.

Unlike the main comparison, the grid does not use common random
numbers. Each cell is drawn from its own seed, so the schedules
within a structure are independent samples. For each cell we
report the analytic relative efficiency beside the empirical one.
The empirical value is the ratio of two independent variance
estimates. Its 95\% interval uses the \(F\) distribution with
\(R - 1\) and \(R - 1\) degrees of freedom. Here \(R\) is the number of
converged replications. This interval assumes that the estimates
are close to normal. The simulation serves to check agreement
with the analytic values and the accuracy of the model-based SE.
It is not an independent test of the direction of the efficiency
difference, which the closed form already settles. The grid
assumes complete data. The replication count per cell is lower
than the main comparison's 2000. This keeps the run on one
core to about
7
minutes. \texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_full\_factorial.R}
regenerates the grid.

\subsection{Simulation procedure}\label{simulation-procedure}

The simulation functions are \texttt{simulate\_trial()}, \texttt{run\_one\_sim()},
\texttt{run\_paired\_simulation()}, and \texttt{analytic\_gls\_se()}. They live in
\texttt{R/} as documented, unit-tested package functions
(\texttt{inst/tinytest/}) and not inline in this document. As a result,
they can be reused for the scenario extension and audited
independently of the manuscript.
For each of 2000 Monte Carlo replications,
\texttt{run\_paired\_simulation()}:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Generates 100 subjects, randomized 1:1 to treatment
  and control. It draws one shared set of subject-level random
  effects \((b_{0i}, b_{1i})\). It also draws one shared set of
  residual errors \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) per subject-visit index
  \(j = 1, \dots, 5\).
\item
  Builds the equal-spacing and clustered-spacing datasets from
  \emph{those same draws}. That is, the \(j\)-th random effect and
  residual realization is reused across designs for the \(j\)-th
  visit and is not redrawn. These are common random numbers
  (CRN). They pair the two designs within each replication. They
  also substantially reduce the Monte Carlo error of the
  \emph{difference} between designs, relative to two independent
  simulation arms.
\item
  Fits a linear mixed model (random intercepts and slopes) to each
  design's dataset using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) via
  \texttt{nlme::lme()}.
\item
  Extracts the point estimate, standard error, and \(p\)-value for
  \(\hat{\beta}_3\) for each design.
\end{enumerate}

For each design we compute the mean estimate (bias), the empirical
SD of \(\hat{\beta}_3\), the mean model-based SE, empirical power
(\(p < 0.05\)), and 95\% CI coverage. Each has a Monte Carlo
standard error (MCSE) computed following\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{18}}.
The MCSE of the empirical SD is \(\text{SE} / \sqrt{2(R-1)}\). The
MCSE of a proportion (power, coverage) is
\(\sqrt{\hat p (1-\hat p) / R}\). The MCSE of the bias is
\(\text{SE} / \sqrt{R}\). Here \(R\) is the number of converged
replications. We also report a paired bootstrap 95\% interval for
the relative efficiency (ratio of empirical variances). The
bootstrap resamples replication indices. This respects the CRN
pairing.

The driver script \texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_simulation.R} runs this
procedure. It saves the raw per-replication estimates, the
summary statistics with MCSEs, the analytic SEs, and the
bootstrap interval. These go to a single versioned artifact,
\texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sim\_results.rds}. This document reads
that artifact directly. It does not re-simulate on every render
or rely on the knitr cache to stay synchronized with the reported
numbers. The script sets the seed
20260305 before the loop. To regenerate:
\texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_simulation.R\ 2000}. The results below
used 2000 replications, R version 4.6.1 (2026-06-24),
and \texttt{nlme} 3.1.169. The run took
176 seconds
(single core, no parallelization).

\subsection{Monotone dropout (analytic)}\label{monotone-dropout-analytic}

The comparison above assumes complete data. The clustered schedule
leaves a 16-month gap, and 60\%
of its \(S_{tt}\) comes from the last two visits. Dropout could
therefore cost it more than equal spacing. To check this, we
computed the expected GLS information under monotone dropout that
is missing completely at random (MCAR). Each dropout pattern
contributes its own information matrix, weighted by its expected
frequency. With incomplete data, \(\tau_0\) and \(\tau_{01}\) do
enter the calculation, and we use the base-case values. We let
dropout follow a constant hazard in calendar time. The cumulative
dropout by month 24 ranges from 0 to
40\%. The function is
\texttt{analytic\_gls\_se\_dropout()} in \texttt{R/design.R}.

The relative efficiency of the clustered design falls steadily as
dropout rises. It is 1.063, 1.042, 1.019, 0.993, 0.963 for cumulative dropout of
0\%, 10\%, 20\%, 30\%, 40\%. Equal spacing becomes the more efficient
design once cumulative dropout exceeds about
27\%. This threshold depends on
the MCAR assumption, the constant-hazard retention curve, and the
base-case variance components. It is not a general result. We
have not examined dropout that depends on outcomes. Even so, the
small complete-data advantage of the clustered design should not
be relied on alone. A dropout calculation of this kind for the
trial at hand is needed.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

For complete, balanced data under the random-intercept,
random-slope model, the visit schedule affects the precision of
the slope contrast only through \(S_{tt}\). The comparison of
schedules is therefore a matter of arithmetic. The clustered
schedule studied here gains
6.3\% in efficiency over
equal spacing at the base-case parameters. The unconstrained
optimum would gain
11.6\%, and the
best schedule with a two-month minimum gap would gain
7.8\%. The gain is
largest when the random-slope variance is small relative to
\(\sigma^2 / S_{tt}\). The relative efficiency reaches
1.38 under compound symmetry,
which is the \(\tau_1 = 0\) limit. We have not compared this CS
result with that of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkensOptimalTimepointsClinical2005}{1}} in
detail. Their mean model
may differ from ours in its treatment of the baseline group
difference.

The paired simulation agrees with the exact calculation. Its
main value lies elsewhere, however. REML estimation of the
variance components cannot change the point estimate here, so the
simulation is a check on inference. Coverage and model-based SEs
were close to nominal for both designs. In every cell of the Monte
Carlo grid the model-based SE was close to the empirical SE. The
grid itself used independent random numbers
across schedules. At its replication count it could confirm the
direction of the advantage only where that advantage is large.

Several caveats warrant mention. First, dropout matters more than
the complete-data comparison suggests. Under MCAR dropout with a
constant hazard, equal spacing overtakes the clustered schedule
once cumulative dropout passes about
27\%. Dropout that depends on the
outcome was not examined. A 16-month gap without visits may also
be unacceptable for safety monitoring and retention. Second, every
model in the Monte Carlo grid was fitted with the covariance
family that generated the data. Misspecification of the covariance
was not examined. Neither were heterogeneous residual variances.
Third, we considered only a linear treatment effect. A design with
a long mid-study gap cannot detect curvature in that gap.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkens2007polynomial}{12}} showed that optimal designs differ for
polynomial effects of higher order.

In conclusion, three points merit emphasis. First, consider a
linear slope contrast with complete data. The relative efficiency
of any two schedules follows from their corrected sums of squares
and the ratio \(\tau_1^2 / \sigma^2\). A simulation is not needed to compare
them. Second, the gain from boundary clustering is modest under
realistic random-slope variance. In our base case it is
6.3\%, and MCAR dropout of
about 27\% by study end erases
it. Third, a move away from equal spacing should rest on a
calculation that includes the expected dropout pattern. It should
also allow for the need for interim visits. The maximin D-optimal framework of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ouwens2002maximin}{4}} and the Bayesian approach of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-abebe2014bayesian}{14}}
provide starting points for designs that allow for parameter
uncertainty.
